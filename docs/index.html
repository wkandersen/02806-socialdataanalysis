Chicago Crime Scape

Investigating crime and socioeconomic indicators in Chicago

This page presents a small visual story built from social data from the city of Chicago, United States. Use it to explore how crime and socioeconomic patterns are correlated across the city, and to understand what environments feed an active criminal space. The story is built as a three step rocket; first have a look at the the state of the city from a social perspective - ask yourself how the people are doing. Then we move on to the crime patterns of the city - where does crime happen and what differentiates the different crime types? Finally, we will put the picture together and analyze where the two data sets are correlated, and where they are not.
The story will walk you through the analysis of how the socioeconomic state of a city can tell you about the crime and safety of its residents.

Note on data: These visualizations represent police incident reports, not necessarily the total volume of crime. Shifts in trends may reflect changes in CDP enforcement priorities, city policy, or reporting protocols rather than a change in underlying activity.
Similarly, the socioeconomic indicators are estimates, which should be taken into account when interpreting the data. Finally, the population data used to calculate crime rates per capita is based on 2010 data, which may not perfectly reflect the population distribution during the period of crime data collection (2008-2012).

Data Sources

The data used in this project is sourced from the City of Chicago's open data portal, which provides a wide range of datasets related to the city's operations, demographics, and public safety. Specifically, the crime data is derived from the "Crimes - 2001 to Present" dataset, which contains detailed records of reported crimes in Chicago, including the type of crime, location, and date. We only used data from 2008 to 2012 as the socioeconomic indicators only cover this period. The socioeconomic indicators are sourced from the "Hardship Index by Community Area" dataset, which provides a composite index measuring various socioeconomic factors such as poverty, unemployment, and education levels across different community areas in Chicago. Additionally, population data for the community areas was obtained from a publicly available dataset on GitHub, which compiles demographic information for the city in 2010 and 2020, allowing for the calculation of crime rates per capita. We used the 2010 population data to align with the period of crime data collection.
