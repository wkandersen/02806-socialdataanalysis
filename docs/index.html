Socioeconomic Indicators

Lets take a moment to look at the socioeconomic state of Chicago. To measure this, we will look at the Census Data (CityOfChicago.org) collected by the city of Chicago. This dataset contains a variety of socioeconomic indicators for each of the 77 community areas in Chicago, including the Hardship Index, which is a composite measure of socioeconomic hardship based on factors such as unemployment, poverty, and education levels. The socioeconomic data provides a better understanding of how life is for the residents of Chicago, and how it may relate to crime patterns in the city. N.B. Looking at the visualizations above, we can see how the various socioeconomic indicators are correlated to the hardship index. It is clear that a high unemployment rate, a high poverty rate, and a low per capita income are all associated with a high hardship index. Geographically, we can see that the areas with the highest hardship index are located in the south and west sides of Chicago, while the areas with the lowest hardship index are located in the north side, near the coast, of Chicago.

Interpreting the Crime Landscape

Now that we have an understanding of the socioeconomic landscape of Chicago, we can start to look at how crime patterns are distributed across the city, and how they may relate to the socioeconomic indicators we just explored. The following visualizations show the distribution of different types of crime across the community areas in Chicago, as well as how they correlate with the hardship index. We will look at some more crime specific cases related to socioeconomic hardship, and try to interpret how the crime landscape of Chicago is shaped by the socioeconomic conditions of its residents.

Hardship Index vs Crime Count

This visualization shows the relationship between the hardship index and crime count across different community areas in Chicago. We can see that the R² between the hardship index and crime count is only 0.138, which indicates a weak correlation between the two variables. This suggests that while there may be some relationship between socioeconomic hardship and crime, it is not a strong one, and other factors may be at play in determining crime patterns in Chicago.

Theft Distribution vs Socioeconomic Indicators

This scatter plot shows the relationship between the rate of theft and two socioeconomic indicators, the first being the percentage of the population under 18 or over 64, and the second being the per capita income, across different community areas in Chicago. The R² between the theft rate and the percentage of the population under 18 or over 64 is 0.327 indicating that the demographic age distrubtion can explain around 32.7% of the variation in theft rates. Looking at the plot, there is a negative correlation between the two variables, which suggests that areas with a higher percentage of the population under 18 or over 64 tend to have a lower rate of theft. The R² between the theft rate and per capita income is 0.285, indicating that per capita income can explain around 28.5% of the variation in theft rates. The plot shows a positive correlation between the two variables, which suggests that areas with a higher per capita income tend to have a higher rate of theft. While there are some correlations between the theft rate and the socioeconomic indicators, it is important to note that these relationships are not necessarily causal, and other factors may be at play in determining crime patterns in Chicago, specfically we are unable to determine if people from poorer areas are committing crimes in richer areas, or if the crime is being committed by people from the richer areas.
