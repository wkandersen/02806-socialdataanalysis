Socioeconomic Indicators

Lets take a moment to look at the socioeconomic state of Chicago. To measure this, we will look at the Census Data (CityOfChicago.org) collected by the city of Chicago. This dataset contains a variety of socioeconomic indicators for each of the 77 community areas in Chicago, including the Hardship Index, which is a composite measure of socioeconomic hardship based on factors such as unemployment, poverty, and education levels. The socioeconomic data provides a better understanding of how life is for the residents of Chicago, and how it may relate to crime patterns in the city. N.B. Looking at the visualizations above, we can see how the various socioeconomic indicators are correlated with the hardship index. The hardship index map shows that the highest hardship scores are concentrated in the West and South sides of Chicago, while the lowest hardship levels are primarily found in the North side, especially along the lakefront. This fits with the per capita income map, which shows a clear geographic pattern where the highest income areas are located in the North and Northeast, and lower-income communities are found in the West and South. A similar pattern appears in the poverty and unemployment maps, where many of the same areas with lower income also experience higher poverty and unemployment rates. Since the hardship index is constructed using measures such as income, unemployment, and poverty, these overlapping patterns are expected and demonstrate strong consistency between the index and its underlying socioeconomic components across Chicago community areas.

Correlation between Hardship and Crime types

Finally, we can look at the correlation between the Hardship Index and the other socioeconomic indicators across the community areas in Chicago.


The crime correlation plot shows how different types of crime are associated with the hardship index across Chicago community areas. Overall, there is a clear pattern where many violent and public-order crimes tend to be more common in higher-hardship neighborhoods, while several property-related or non-violent offenses show weaker or even slightly negative relationships. On the negative side, crimes such as deceptive practices, theft, and some narcotic-related violations are more weakly associated with lower hardship areas, although these relationships are not very strong. Most other crimes cluster around small to moderate correlations, indicating more mixed or location-dependent patterns. In contrast, many of the strongest positive relationships are seen in more serious crimes such as homicide, weapons violations, battery, assault, and narcotics-related offenses. These tend to be more concentrated in areas with higher hardship index values, suggesting a strong geographical overlap between socioeconomic disadvantage and more severe forms of crime.

It is important to note that these results show correlation, not causation. While crime and hardship clearly co-occur in many of the same neighborhoods, this does not mean that one directly causes the other. Instead, both may be influenced by shared underlying factors such as economic opportunity, population density, policing practices, historical segregation, and neighborhood infrastructure. Additionally, differences in reporting rates and enforcement intensity across neighborhoods may also affect the observed relationships.
